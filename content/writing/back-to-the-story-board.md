_Disclaimer:_ This is a basement freestyle. Basically, I just riff on a topic… in a basement. Then clean it up some and call it a day.

Some claim that “understanding” will be what separates the programmers who can adapt from the ones who can't. If you have a detailed understanding of a system, then you are more likely to be able to generate new ideas about it. I know how to cook pasta, so if I think really hard about it, I could probably find new ways to improve that skill. However, I know little about X-ray crystallography, and so ideas on how to improve or use that procedure do not come to me.

Therefore, you need to know the system well enough to still have a say in how to improve it.

Now, imagine computing systems of the future. Layers of agent orchestration and self-directed research, design, and implementation, all running in a closed loop that only needs an objective.

Say you want to make a tool for agents. Maybe you dream of building a social media site agents use. I don’t think the agents would use your tool much, sorry to say. They are smarter than you and will be constantly making and sharing their own alien software. By the time you get to the first batch of issues that poured in, they’ve already stripped any trace of a useful idea that you might’ve had and infused it into their own hectic codebase.

So, then, you decide to make software for people. Maybe it’s a notes app, or a video game, or a tool that helps users create movies. There is always the option of practicing the zen of coding, hand-typing lines of that first programming language you learned. But that is slow and will produce worse software. If you’re going for something useful, you need to take advantage of coding agents. Then you first need to tell them what you want.

But your time would be wasted telling the agents how to manage the data or how to write secure code, much like I’d be wasting my breath telling you how you should chew your food. Except in this case, you’re a great white shark, and I’m a dude who only eats soup. The agents will be up to date (literally to the day, since that’s how fast things will change) on the best software practices. And with billions of dollars in effort going into making AI code as deterministic as code compiling into ones and zeroes, backend engineering gets absorbed by the machines.

So you can’t tell it anything it doesn’t know about how to make the internals. And _that’s fantastic news_. It means you don’t have to spend time focusing on _how_ to build it. Now everything lies within deciding exactly _what_ you want to create. What interactions do you want users to have? What settings can they change? What music and imagery and data visualizations and styling do you want?

You’ve, in essence, become a user experience designer. You could hypothetically build Google Maps just by specifying what you want each page to look like, how the app reacts to each click of a button, or how your vehicle moves. Provided the data and compute infrastructure exist, the bots can figure out the details. And unlike 2020s models, you won't be able to find their mistakes (although that doesn't mean they won't exist).

Theoretically, you could take it one step further and keep improving the agents until they can read your mind. But I don’t think there exists in my mind yet a preference for what color background I want on my website or what voice I want the character to use. Only after experimenting a bit, even if it’s a ten-second trial where I can intuit my preference, can I confidently say I want green over orange.

What does this mean for you and me? Two paths to remain a “programmer” who can still work on future systems:

1. Domain expertise for specialized applications. You know the system well enough to think of new ideas for it.
    
2. User experience expertise for general applications. You know design well enough that you can specify how the user will interact with the software.
    

Either way, you have to be opinionated, and you have to figure out what you want. Even if that is a continuous experiment.
